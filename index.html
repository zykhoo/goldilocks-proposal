ICLR 2027 Workshop proposal

Goldilocks’ Workshop on Finding “Just Right” in AI

Agentic AI systems combine models, reasoning procedures, tools, memory, retrieval and human input. Their performance often peaks at configurations that are neither too little nor too much, but just right. This workshop studies how to identify, understand and optimize for these Goldilocks zones.

Goldilocks zones

We use the term Goldilocks zones, after the story of Goldilocks and the Three Bears, for sweet spots in agentic AI systems where a combination of configurations (model scale, training data, hyperparameters, training procedures, agent harnesses and so on) is just right and gives unexpectedly strong performance. They arise in three ways.

Saturation

More is not always better.

Increasing resource investment, such as in reasoning, training or human intervention, eventually yields diminishing gains or even degrades performance beyond a certain point.

In agentic reasoning, extra inference-time compute brings diminishing gains at rising cost. The Goldilocks zone is a reasoning budget that balances task performance against inference cost.

Allocation

A fixed budget, optimally split.

Limited resources, such as reasoning tokens and inference GPUs, must be optimally distributed across multiple sub-agents, training stages or system components under a fixed budget.

In post-training agentic models, a fixed annotation budget is divided between supervised fine-tuning and reinforcement learning. The Goldilocks zone is the split that gives near-optimal downstream performance.

Synergy

The whole exceeds its parts.

Interactions among the components of an agentic system, such as models, agent harnesses and tools, produce gains beyond what any single component can achieve on its own.

In agentic autoresearch, the flexible exploration of autonomous agents complements the structure and reliability of traditional search methods. The Goldilocks zone is the set of configurations that makes the most of this.

Motivation

Agentic AI research is moving towards systems that combine models, reasoning procedures, tools, memory, retrieval, agents, human input, and training and inference pipelines. As the number of interacting components grows, effective configurations become harder to find, and findings from one system may not carry over to another.

Research on saturation, allocation and synergy currently appears as isolated ablations or heuristics. Papers often report the best configuration without describing the performance landscape around it, the mechanisms behind the optimum, the conditions under which it holds, or whether it transfers. This makes it hard to recognize recurring patterns and to develop general principles and methods.

The workshop brings together researchers who observe Goldilocks zones empirically and those who develop theory and optimization methods to explain and navigate them.

Aims

The workshop aims to advance three open problems:

  1. Understanding where and why saturation, allocation trade-offs and synergy arise, rather than reporting a single best configuration.
  2. Determining whether these zones carry over across tasks, model scales and systems.
  3. Developing sample-efficient methods, including agents, that locate, track or shift these zones under limited budgets.

Call for papers

The workshop brings together empirical and theoretical researchers who work on agents, optimization and beyond. Our goal is to identify where and why Goldilocks problems arise, and to develop methods for finding and optimizing Goldilocks zones.

We welcome both Goldilocks problems in agents and agents for solving Goldilocks problems across AI research. That includes empirical problems in models and pipelines that are not themselves agentic, and theoretical problems, for example in game theory or in combining classical optimization with agentic workflows. Categories may overlap, and submissions may address empirical questions, theoretical questions, or both.

Goldilocks statement

Please include a one-sentence Goldilocks statement in the abstract or introduction that explains how the paper relates to the topics below. An existing sentence can serve this purpose. For example:

“This paper investigates [problem or method] in [setting], with a focus on [saturation, allocation, or synergy].”
“This paper uses agents to investigate or solve [empirical or theoretical problem] involving [saturation, allocation, synergy, or a related topic].”

These examples are illustrative, and any wording that clearly explains the connection is fine.

Topics

Topics include, but are not limited to, the following.

We particularly encourage submissions that advance our understanding of Goldilocks problems or develop methods for addressing them. What gives rise to saturation, allocation trade-offs or synergy? How do these phenomena vary across models, tasks or settings? How can they be characterized, explained or optimized? How can agents help investigate or solve them?

Saturation in agents

Empirical. Along which axes (inference-time compute, training duration, context or memory size, model scale, autonomy, human intervention) do returns flatten or reverse, and what failures appear past the useful range?

Theoretical. Under what assumptions does such a range exist, and how can optimal stopping or adaptive methods locate and track it as tasks, models or environments change?

Allocation in agents

Empirical. Under a fixed budget, how should compute, data, rollouts, context tokens, tool calls or human effort be divided across agents, tasks, stages and components? How do good allocations shift with capability, difficulty or budget?

Theoretical. What principles and algorithms characterize optimal allocations, and what scaling laws or invariances let them transfer across model scales, tasks or budgets?

Synergy in agents

Empirical. Which combinations of agents, models, tools, memory, retrieval, search or human input are complementary or antagonistic? Which ablations or factorial experiments separate real interaction effects from simply stronger components?

Theoretical. How can complementarity or interference be formalized, and how can an optimizer find productive combinations without evaluating them all?

Characterization and validation

Which signals (uncertainty, diversity, latency, marginal gains) show a system entering or leaving a Goldilocks zone? How can its geometry be characterized, and how can real structure be told apart from optima caused by noise or small evaluation budgets?

Generalization and redesign

How stable are Goldilocks zones across agent configurations, model scales, datasets, domains, distribution shift and deployment constraints? Which sweet spots transfer? Can design shift, widen or remove their boundaries, for instance by delaying saturation or enabling new synergies?

Efficient optimization

How can multi-fidelity, Bayesian or bi-level optimization, or adaptive experimental design, locate Goldilocks zones when configurations are expensive, noisy, high-dimensional, combinatorial or interacting? What makes these zones identifiable from limited observations?

Agents for empirical and theoretical discovery

How can agents propose hypotheses, run experiments and analyze results to discover and explain saturation, allocation and synergy in training, data selection, inference and model composition? How can they form conjectures and develop proofs or counterexamples for problems like resource allocation or equilibrium analysis? How are findings reproduced and verified?

Agents for optimization across AI

How can agents, alone or with structured search, find, track or improve Goldilocks zones, including outside agentic systems? How do they compare with non-agentic methods at matched budgets, and what guarantees hold for search efficiency or solution quality?

Submission and review

  • Format and review: Submissions use the ICLR 2027 template.
  • Novel or ongoing work: Papers published at archival venues, including the ICLR 2027 main conference, will not be considered.
  • AI policy: All submissions must comply with the ICLR 2027 AI Policy for Authors. AI assistance is permitted, but short and tiny papers must be written primarily by humans.
  • Non-archival: Accepted Main, Tiny and Collaboration Track papers will be hosted publicly on this website and on OpenReview, as a record of the ideas and collaborations developed through the workshop.

Awards and support

Funding for awards, travel and refreshments is already secured. Best paper awards in the Main and Tiny Papers tracks total US$1,000. Incentive awards in the Collaboration Track total US$500. Travel grants of US$3,000 support attendees who need them, including young researchers. A further US$1,000 covers the lunch and coffee breaks after the roundtables.

If the workshop is accepted, submissions will open on OpenReview on 1 December 2026, using the ICLR 2027 author kit.
