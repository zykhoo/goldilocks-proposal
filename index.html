Goldilocks’ Workshop on Finding “Just Right” in Agentic AI

Model scale, data, training, inference budgets and agent harnesses interact. Some combinations land in a sweet spot and work surprisingly well. This workshop is about finding those spots, understanding them, and optimizing for them.

ICLR 2027, San Francisco. Full day, in person, on 29 or 30 April 2027.

Read the call for papers See key dates

Three bears, three zones

A Goldilocks zone shows up in three recurring ways. Each one asks a different question about the same system.

Saturation

Papa Bear’s porridge: hotter is not always better.

Spending more on reasoning, training or human intervention eventually stops paying off, and can start to hurt. The zone is the budget that balances performance against cost.

Extra inference-time compute that no longer improves agentic reasoning.

Allocation

Mama Bear’s chair: how tall can it be and still leave enough wood for Papa and Baby?

A fixed budget of tokens, GPUs, annotations or human effort has to be divided across sub-agents or stages. The zone is the split that gets near-optimal results.

Dividing an annotation budget between supervised fine-tuning and RL in post-training.

Synergy

Baby Bear’s bed: mattress, pillow and quilt only work together.

Models, harnesses and tools combine so that the whole beats any part on its own. The zone is the configuration that makes the most of their complementary strengths.

Autonomous research agents paired with structured Bayesian optimization.

Why a workshop on this, now

Agentic systems now combine models, reasoning procedures, tools, memory, retrieval, human input, and training and inference pipelines. As the number of interacting parts grows, good configurations get harder to find, and what works in one system often fails to transfer to another.

Findings about saturation, allocation and synergy are scattered across papers as isolated ablations and heuristics. Papers report the best setting, but rarely the shape of the optimum, why it exists, where its edges are, or whether it carries over. That makes recurring patterns hard to see.

This workshop brings these observations together, and pairs researchers who see Goldilocks zones empirically with those building theory and optimization methods to explain and navigate them.

What comes out of it

Contributed papers, invited talks and two roundtables feed into a public Goldilocks Research Agenda: a lasting reference on the mechanisms behind saturation, allocation and synergy, and on how to find and optimize for these zones.

Two roundtables

Finding Goldilocks problems looks at recurring phenomena, their causes and open questions. Finding Goldilocks solutions looks at methods to explain, locate, navigate or widen these zones. Each starts with 4 to 6 invited authors, and everyone is welcome to join.

Call for papers

We welcome both Goldilocks problems in agents and agents for solving Goldilocks problems across AI research. That includes empirical problems in models and pipelines that aren’t agentic themselves, and theoretical problems, for example in game theory or in combining classical optimization with agentic workflows.

When you submit, choose one of two categories: Empirical (identifying and characterizing Goldilocks phenomena) or Optimization & Theory (explaining and navigating Goldilocks zones). Empirical posters present in the morning, Optimization & Theory in the afternoon, so you can visit the other session.

Reviews focus on clarity, rigor and novelty. For empirical work, we especially value papers that characterize the structure around a zone, not just report a best configuration. The workshop is non-archival; accepted papers stay on the website and OpenReview.

Begin with a Goldilocks statement

Every submission opens with one sentence naming what is being varied, what goes wrong with too little and too much of it, and the middle ground the paper studies.

“Too little [X] leads to [outcome A], whereas too much [X] leads to [outcome B]; this paper investigates how to identify or achieve an intermediate level that [desired outcome].”

Topics include, but are not limited to

Saturation in agents

Empirical. Along which axes (inference-time compute, training duration, context or memory size, model scale, autonomy, human intervention) do returns flatten or reverse, and what failures appear past the useful range?

Theoretical. Under what assumptions does such a range exist, and how can optimal stopping or adaptive methods locate and track it as tasks, models or environments change?

Allocation in agents

Empirical. Under a fixed budget, how should compute, data, rollouts, context tokens, tool calls or human effort be divided across agents, tasks, stages and components? How do good allocations shift with capability, difficulty or budget?

Theoretical. What principles and algorithms characterize optimal allocations, and what scaling laws or invariances let them transfer across model scales, tasks or budgets?

Synergy in agents

Empirical. Which combinations of agents, models, tools, memory, retrieval, search or human input are complementary or antagonistic? Which ablations or factorial experiments separate real interaction effects from simply stronger components?

Theoretical. How can complementarity or interference be formalized, and how can an optimizer find productive combinations without evaluating them all?

Characterization and validation

Which signals (uncertainty, diversity, latency, marginal gains) show a system entering or leaving a Goldilocks zone? How can its geometry be characterized, and how can real structure be told apart from optima caused by noise or small evaluation budgets?

Generalization and redesign

How stable are Goldilocks zones across agent configurations, model scales, datasets, domains, distribution shift and deployment constraints? Which sweet spots transfer? Can design shift, widen or remove their boundaries, for instance by delaying saturation or enabling new synergies?

Efficient optimization

How can multi-fidelity, Bayesian or bi-level optimization, or adaptive experimental design, locate Goldilocks zones when configurations are expensive, noisy, high-dimensional, combinatorial or interacting? What makes these zones identifiable from limited observations?

Agents for empirical and theoretical discovery

How can agents propose hypotheses, run experiments and analyze results to discover and explain saturation, allocation and synergy in training, data selection, inference and model composition? How can they form conjectures and develop proofs or counterexamples for problems like resource allocation or equilibrium analysis? How are findings reproduced and verified?

Agents for optimization across AI

How can agents, alone or with structured search, find, track or improve Goldilocks zones, including outside agentic systems? How do they compare with non-agentic methods at matched budgets, and what guarantees hold for search efficiency or solution quality?
